%% ── Abstract ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
Parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods such as LoRA can substantially improve
factual prediction accuracy, yet how these changes propagate across transformer
layers remains poorly understood.
We study \emph{where} in the forward pass LoRA writes factual knowledge by
applying logit-lens analysis and causal activation patching to Pythia-410m
fine-tuned on CounterFact facts.
We distinguish four conditions ordered by how much prior signal the base model
has: facts it already knows (\emph{known}), facts whose correct answer appears
in the top-5 but not top-1 (\emph{latent}), real facts with no top-5 signal
(\emph{unknown}), and entirely invented facts (\emph{synthetic}).
Causal activation patching reveals a strict ordering: known facts flip at
layer~0 (median), latent facts at an intermediate depth, unknown facts at
layer~14, and synthetic facts at layer~19 out of 24.
Logit-lens trajectories confirm that LoRA shifts synthetic predictions
\emph{later} ($+3.1$ layers) and known predictions \emph{earlier} ($-2.7$
layers).
Rank ablation shows that higher-rank adapters increase the number of facts
that can be encoded but do not change which layers encode them.
All gains come at a steep locality cost: only 20.8\% of neighboring-fact
predictions are preserved for known conditions, dropping to 7.5\% for unknown.
\end{abstract}

%% ── 1. Introduction ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Introduction}

Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA; \citealt{hu2022lora}) has become one of the
dominant methods for adapting large language models to new tasks.
By injecting small trainable matrices at each attention layer, LoRA achieves
competitive accuracy while updating fewer than 1\% of model parameters.
However, the improvement in final-layer predictions tells us little about
\emph{how} the model arrives at those predictions.

A natural question is whether LoRA simply amplifies an answer that was already
latent in the network, or whether it installs the answer from scratch in the
final layers.
Prior work on language adapters found that adapted predictions often shift
toward the target predominantly in the final layers
\citep{alabi2024hidden}.
Whether LoRA for factual knowledge exhibits the same late-layer pattern --- and
whether this depends on the novelty of the fact being injected --- is unknown.

We ask: \textbf{How does LoRA fine-tuning on factual knowledge affect the
evolution of predictions across transformer layers, and does this differ
along a spectrum from facts the model already knows, to facts where the
correct answer is latent but suppressed, to facts it has no signal for,
to entirely invented facts?}

To answer this, we fine-tune Pythia-410m-deduped on a stratified subset of the
CounterFact benchmark \citep{meng2022locating} and analyse the resulting model
using logit lens \citep{logitlens2020}, tuned lens \citep{belrose2023eliciting},
and causal activation patching.
We run patching at every training checkpoint (not only the final model) to
track \emph{when} causal structure emerges during training.
We supplement this with a rank ablation ($r \in \{4,8,16,32\}$) and a locality
audit on 10{,}000 neighborhood prompts.

Our main finding is that LoRA's encoding depth forms a strict spectrum aligned
with knowledge novelty: known (layer 0) $<$ latent $<$ unknown (layer 14) $<$
synthetic (layer 19).
This ordering is stable across all training steps and adapter ranks; higher rank
increases how many facts are encoded, but not where.

%% ── 2. Related Work ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Parameter-efficient fine-tuning.}
\citet{hu2022lora} introduce LoRA, which freezes the pretrained weight matrix
$W$ and learns a low-rank decomposition $\Delta W = BA$ with $B \in
\mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$, $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}$.
The effective change to each layer's output is $\alpha / r \cdot BAx$, where
$\alpha$ is a fixed scaling factor.
LoRA has been applied to language modelling, instruction following, and
knowledge editing, and is the basis of most modern fine-tuning pipelines.

\paragraph{Interpreting intermediate representations.}
The logit lens \citep{logitlens2020} decodes intermediate hidden states through
the unembedding head to obtain a vocabulary distribution at each layer, enabling
inspection of how predictions evolve through the forward pass.
\citet{belrose2023eliciting} extend this with a tuned lens: a learned affine
probe per layer that predicts the final-layer output distribution more
accurately than the raw logit lens.
We use both, reporting the logit lens as the primary metric to avoid
conflating lens-specific effects with LoRA-induced changes.

\paragraph{Adapters and layer dynamics.}
\citet{alabi2024hidden} study how adapter modules and LoRA affect the
hidden-state trajectory, finding that adapted predictions shift toward the
target predominantly in the final layers for language-transfer tasks.
We test whether the same late-layer dominance appears for factual knowledge,
and whether it holds across knowledge types.

\paragraph{Factual knowledge in LLMs.}
\citet{meng2022locating} show through causal tracing that factual associations
in GPT models are primarily stored in mid-layer MLP modules.
Our activation patching adapts their methodology: we patch hidden states from
the fine-tuned model into the base model to find the earliest layer at which
the LoRA update causally determines the output.

%% ── 3. Methodology ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Model and Dataset}
We use \textbf{Pythia-410m-deduped} \citep{biderman2023pythia}, a 24-layer
decoder-only transformer with hidden size 1{,}024.
Facts are drawn from the \textbf{CounterFact} benchmark
\citep{meng2022locating}, which provides 19{,}728 subject--relation--object
triples along with paraphrase prompts for each fact.
We retain only facts whose target answer is a single token under the model's
tokenizer, yielding 17{,}606 usable facts.

\subsection{Experimental Conditions}
We define four conditions ordered by how much prior signal the base model has
for the correct answer:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Known (rank 1):} The base model already predicts the correct
    answer as its top-1 token; 1{,}232 facts qualify (7.0\%).
  \item \textbf{Latent (rank 2--5):} The fact is real and the correct answer
    appears in the model's top-5 predictions, but is not the top-1.
    The representation exists but is suppressed by a competing prediction.
  \item \textbf{Unknown (rank $>$5):} The fact is real but the correct answer
    has no top-5 signal at baseline.
    The model lacks the association entirely.
  \item \textbf{Synthetic:} Pseudo-entity facts created by pairing real
    relation templates (e.g.\ \emph{``\{subject\} was born in''}) with a
    randomly generated subject name and a real object from that relation.
    By construction the base model has never encountered the triple.
\end{itemize}
This four-way split lets us test a spectrum hypothesis: if LoRA's encoding
depth is determined by how absent the knowledge is, we predict
$\text{known} < \text{latent} < \text{unknown} < \text{synthetic}$
in causal first-flip layer.
We stratify-sample up to 500 facts per condition and 200 synthetic facts,
capping each relation at 25\% to prevent any single relation from dominating.
Paraphrase prompts are held out and never used in training.

\subsection{LoRA Fine-Tuning}
We attach LoRA adapters ($r=16$, $\alpha=32$, dropout $0.05$) to the
\texttt{query\_key\_value} projections of all attention layers (1.57M trainable
parameters; 0.39\% of total).
Training uses the cross-entropy loss on the answer token only (prompt tokens
masked), AdamW with learning rate $10^{-4}$, batch size 16, for 5 epochs (375
optimizer steps).
We save adapter checkpoints every 50 steps.

\subsection{Layer-wise Analysis}
For each adapter checkpoint we run the full forward pass with
\texttt{output\_hidden\_states=True} and apply the logit lens (frozen final
layer-norm $+$ unembedding head) to every intermediate hidden state at the last
token position.
We record acc@1, mean log-probability, and the first layer at which the correct
answer appears as top-1 (\emph{first-layer-of-appearance}).
The \emph{base model's} tuned lens is loaded from the AlignmentResearch hub and
kept frozen across all variants, ensuring that any change in its readout is
attributable to LoRA, not to changes in the probe.

\subsection{Causal Activation Patching}
For each fact and each layer $\ell \in \{1,\ldots,24\}$, we patch the LoRA
model's residual stream after block $\ell$ into the base model via a forward
hook, and record whether the base model's output flips to the target answer.
The \emph{first-flip layer} is the smallest $\ell$ at which the flip occurs.
A first-flip layer near 0 means the answer was already causally present
(LoRA only amplified it); a first-flip layer near 24 means the entire update
lives in the final layers.
We run patching at every training checkpoint to track how causal depth evolves
during fine-tuning.

\subsection{Layer-wise Prediction Evolution}

Table~\ref{tab:main_results} shows the final-layer statistics.
Figure~\ref{fig:delta_logprob} shows how LoRA's gain in answer log-probability
is distributed across the 24 transformer layers.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/fig_delta_logprob.pdf}
  \caption{Mean increase in answer log-probability at each layer
  (LoRA final $-$ base, training prompts).
  Vertical dotted lines mark the median causal first-flip layers
  for unknown (layer~14) and synthetic (layer~19) conditions from patching.}
  \label{fig:delta_logprob}
\end{figure}

The three conditions exhibit qualitatively different shapes.
For \textbf{known} facts, the gain is small in absolute terms ($\Delta = +1.52$
log-prob at the final layer) and rises gradually from the earliest layers,
consistent with a shallow amplification of an already-strong signal.
The first-layer-of-appearance shifts $2.7$ layers earlier (from 21.2 to 18.6),
and paraphrase accuracy jumps from 31.4\% to 79.2\% --- LoRA is making an
existing representation more robust, not writing a new one.

For \textbf{unknown} facts, the gain is near zero through the first half of the
network and rises sharply after layer 14, reaching $+6.0$ log-prob at the final
layer.
The paraphrase accuracy gain ($\Delta = +0.387$) is 81\% as large as the
known-fact gain ($\Delta = +0.478$) despite starting from near zero --- strong
evidence that LoRA is surfacing a latent representation rather than memorising
the training prompt.

For \textbf{synthetic} facts, the log-probability gain stays near zero until
layer~20 and then spikes to $+7.2$, entirely in the final four layers.
The first-layer-of-appearance shifts $+3.1$ layers \emph{later} compared to
the base model, the opposite direction from known facts.
This directly answers our central question: for knowledge with no pre-existing
representation, LoRA installs it as a shallow output bias in the deepest layers.

Table~\ref{tab:patching} and Figure~\ref{fig:patching} confirm the layer-wise
picture causally.
For \textbf{known} facts, all 100 flip at layer 0: the answer is causally
present in the model's input embedding, and the LoRA adapter has not moved it.
For \textbf{unknown} facts, 87/100 flip at a tight median of layer 14 (IQR
visible in Figure~\ref{fig:patching} left panel).
For \textbf{synthetic} facts, 72/100 flip at a median of 19.

The right panel of Figure~\ref{fig:patching} reveals a clean separation between
\emph{where} and \emph{how many}.
Across all 8 training checkpoints (steps 50--375), the first-flip layer for
unknown facts stays near 14 and for synthetic facts near 19 --- the location
does not drift.
What grows monotonically is the count: the number of facts that LoRA has
successfully encoded increases with training, but always at the same layer
depth.
This means \textbf{the encoding layer is a property of the fact type, not of
training progress}.

\subsection{Effect of LoRA Rank}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_rank_ablation.pdf}
  \caption{\emph{Left:} Accuracy vs rank (solid = train prompts, dashed = paraphrases).
  \emph{Right:} Mean first-appearance layer vs rank.
  For synthetic facts, depth stays fixed near layers 21--22 regardless of rank,
  showing that rank controls capacity but not where encoding occurs.}
  \label{fig:rank_ablation}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:rank_ablation} shows that rank governs accuracy (left panel)
but not depth (right panel).
Train accuracy for \textbf{synthetic} facts rises sharply with rank (22.5\% at
$r=4$ to 45.5\% at $r=32$); for \textbf{unknown} facts the gain is even larger
(45.8\% to 75.8\%).
\textbf{Known} facts are already rank-saturated: train accuracy stays 97--99\%
and paraphrase accuracy plateaus at $r=8$ (81.7\%).

The right panel is the key new result.
The mean first-appearance layer for \textbf{synthetic} facts is 22.3 at $r=4$
and 21.6 at $r=32$ --- essentially flat, and 3--4 layers \emph{later} than the
base model's 18.5.
Doubling or quadrupling the rank does not move synthetic facts to earlier
layers.
For \textbf{known} facts the story differs slightly: depth decreases from 19.4
to 18.6 as rank increases, suggesting that larger adapters can slightly earlier-
ise an already-present representation.
The conclusion is that \textbf{rank controls how many facts are encoded, not
where they are encoded} --- the late-layer bias for novel knowledge is
architecturally intrinsic, not a capacity artefact.

Table~\ref{tab:locality} shows that fine-tuning causes severe collateral
disruption.
Only 20.8\% of neighborhood predictions are preserved for the known condition;
for unknown facts the rate drops to 7.5\%.
The unknown condition is far more disruptive despite encoding in middle rather
than final layers: the aggressive mid-layer rewrites required to surface a
suppressed representation collaterally overwrite a wider neighbourhood.
This aligns with prior findings that model editing methods that change
intermediate representations tend to exhibit poor locality
\citep{meng2022locating}.

%% ── 5. Discussion ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Discussion}

Our results paint a consistent picture: \textbf{the layer at which LoRA
encodes a fact is determined by the fact's novelty to the model, not by adapter
capacity or training duration}.
Known facts require only a shallow amplification (layer 0); unknown facts need
a mid-network rewrite (layer 14); synthetic facts must be injected in the
deepest layers (layer 19).

This ordering has a natural interpretation.
The pretrained model has already built a hierarchy of representations for real
facts: the subject entity is resolved in early layers, and its properties are
assembled by mid-network attention.
LoRA on known facts can leverage this: it only needs to increase the weight
of an already-correct answer, which the shallow layers can do.
For unknown facts, the representation exists but is suppressed; LoRA must
intervene at the point where relational information is assembled (mid-network).
For synthetic facts, no representation exists: LoRA must add one from scratch,
and the only layers with enough flexibility to insert a new output signal are
those closest to the vocabulary projection.

The locality results strengthen this picture.
Unknown-fact fine-tuning is the most disruptive ($7.5\%$ preservation) because
modifying mid-network attention broadly affects the representations of nearby
entities.
Synthetic facts, despite being encoded later, are slightly less disruptive
because the final layers have more modular structure.

A limitation of our study is that we fine-tune all three conditions jointly.
Disentangling the interactions between them in future work --- for example by
training on each condition separately and measuring cross-contamination ---
would sharpen these conclusions.
Additionally, extending the analysis to larger models (e.g.\ Pythia-1B or
Pythia-6.9B) would reveal whether the depth ordering is model-size dependent.

%% ── 6. Conclusion ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}

We have shown that LoRA fine-tuning for factual knowledge does not produce a
uniform layer-wise effect.
Instead, the depth at which an update is causally localised is entirely
determined by how novel the fact is to the model.
Patching experiments confirm that known facts are handled from layer 0, unknown
facts from layer 14, and synthetic facts from layer 19 --- and this ordering is
stable across training steps and adapter ranks.
Rank ablation demonstrates that higher capacity yields more facts encoded, but
at the same depth; the first-layer-of-appearance does not move earlier with
rank for synthetic facts.
All conditions suffer severe locality degradation, with unknown fine-tuning
destroying 92.5\% of neighborhood predictions.

These findings suggest that the late-layer dominance reported for language
adapters generalises to factual LoRA fine-tuning, but only for genuinely novel
knowledge.
When fine-tuning to reinforce existing representations, LoRA acts on the
earliest layers and generalises well.
Practitioners who need high locality should prefer known-fact reinforcement
over novel-fact injection and should monitor neighborhood prompts during
training.
